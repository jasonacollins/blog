<p>As for last year, this year’s top book list comprises the best books I <em>have read</em> this year. I haven’t read enough books published in 2011 to be able to apply a decent filter, plus there are many books out there that we should not forget. In no particular order:</p>
<p><a href="http://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/97">Flatland: a romance of many dimensions</a> by Edwin Abbott - Clever, fun satire</p>
<p><a href="/posts/brookss-sex-genes-rock-n-roll.html">Sex, Genes &amp; Rock ‘N’ Roll: How Evolution has Shaped the Modern World</a> by Rob Brooks - While the book is generally a fun read, it makes this list for two specific parts: the discussions of sexual conflict in the context of population and on obesity (<a href="/posts/brookss-sex-genes-rock-n-roll.html">my</a> <a href="/posts/evolution-and-obesity.html">discussion of the obesity chapter</a>).</p>
<p><em>Frankenstein</em> by Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley - One of the classics that lives up to its reputation.</p>
<p><a href="/posts/millers-spent-sex-evolution-and-consumer-behavior.html">Spent: Sex, Evolution and Behavior</a> by Geoffrey Miller - Miller’s analysis of consumer culture under the lens of evolution is the sharpest I have read - and the most fun.</p>
<p><a href="/posts/franks-the-darwin-economy.html">The Darwin Economy: Liberty, Competition, and the Common Good</a> by Robert Frank - While the main point in this book could have been presented as an essay and I disagree with many of the applications, the central concept that competition is not always for the common good, as we can see in evolution, is important.</p>
<p><a href="/posts/fukuyamas-the-origins-of-political-order.html">The Origins of Political Order: From Prehuman Times to the French Revolution</a> by Francis Fukuyama - Fukuyama’s use of kin selection as the foundation to his analysis gives this book a solid foundation lacking from most grand histories (<a href="/posts/fukuyamas-biological-approach.html">some earlier comments</a>).</p>
<p><a href="/posts/veblens-the-theory-of-the-leisure-class.html">Theory of the Leisure Class</a> by Thorstein Veblen - This book was hard to read and at times inconsistent, but it is clear why it is one of those important books for economists to read.</p>
<p><a href="/posts/zimbardos-the-lucifer-effect.html">The Lucifer Effect: Understanding How Good People Turn Evil</a> by Philip Zimbardo - Although these books are in no particular order, I am going to suggest that this is probably the best. It had me thinking like few others (my <a href="/posts/zimbardos-the-lucifer-effect.html">review</a> and <a href="/posts/iq-externalities.html">a later musing</a>).</p>
<p>There are a few books not on this list that were released this year and I have high hopes for - Pinker’s <em>The Better Angels of Our Nature: Why Violence Has Declined</em>, Kahneman’s <a href="/posts/kahnemans-thinking-fast-and-slow.html">Thinking, Fast and Slow</a> and Trivers’s <a href="/posts/triverss-the-folly-of-fools.html">The Folly of Fools: The Logic of Deceit and Self-Deception in Human Life</a>.</p>
