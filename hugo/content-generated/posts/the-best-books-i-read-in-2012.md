<p>As is normally the case, my annual list comprises the best books <em>I have read</em> in the past year, irrespective of their date of release. I read fewer books this year than usual, so I’m drawing from a smaller pool than for the last couple of years (<a href="/posts/top-10-books-in-2010.html">2010</a> and <a href="/posts/best-books-i-read-in-2011.html">2011</a>). Here are my favourite six for 2012 - links are to reviews:</p>
<p><a href="/posts/franks-passions-within-reason.html">Passions Within Reason: The Strategic Role of the Emotions</a> by Robert Frank: A book I should have read a long time ago. I particularly appreciated Frank’s use of path-dependent evolution to develop his model of human behaviour.</p>
<p><a href="/posts/haidts-the-righteous-mind.html">The Righteous Mind: Why Good People Are Divided by Politics and Religion</a> by Jonathan Haidt: At the top of many lists for good reason.</p>
<p><a href="/posts/harfords-adapt-why-success-always-starts-with-failure.html">Adapt: Why Success Always Starts with Failure</a> by Tim Harford: Apart from being interesting and full of reasonable advice, Harford demonstrates a deep understanding of evolutionary processes, which is not often the case in books of this nature.</p>
<p><a href="/posts/kahnemans-thinking-fast-and-slow.html">Thinking, Fast and Slow</a> by Daniel Kahneman: Magnificent. The clear and accessible way that each chapter illustrates a bias or heuristic makes it the best book on rationality and decision-making that I have read.</p>
<p><em>Moby Dick</em> by Herman Melville: The best classic I read this year. Although I could have skipped some of the detours, many are fascinating.</p>
<p><a href="/posts/rubins-darwinian-politics.html">Darwinian Politics: The Evolutionary Origin of Freedom</a> by Paul Rubin: A strong argument for political institutions that maximise freedom.</p>
<p>There are a few books that I read this year that have me in two minds, so I haven’t included them in the above list. I enjoyed Ridley’s <a href="/posts/ridleys-the-rational-optimist.html">The Rational Optimist: How Prosperity Evolves</a>, but despite his claims to the contrary, Ridley stretched the evolutionary metaphor too far in drawing a Panglossian case. Robert Trivers’s <a href="/posts/triverss-the-folly-of-fools.html">The Folly of Fools: The Logic of Deceit and Self-Deception in Human Life</a> would have required a much more thorough editing to make this list. I also enjoyed Kevin Kelly’s <a href="/posts/kellys-what-technology-wants.html">What Technology Wants</a>, despite not buying the central argument.</p>
<p>Of the books I have in my reading pile, I still haven’t got to Pinker’s <em>The Better Angels of Our Nature: Why Violence Has Declined</em>, and I intend to read Flynn’s <a href="/posts/flynns-are-we-getting-smarter.html">Are We Getting Smarter?: Rising IQ in the Twenty-First Century</a> over the Christmas break. Hopefully they will crack next year’s list.</p>
