<p>Yesterday was day one of the <a href="http://msix.com.au/">Marketing Science Ideas Xchange</a> (MSiX). As I mentioned in <a href="/posts/msix-marketing-science-ideas-xchange.html">a previous post</a>, it has been an interesting opportunity to see behavioural science outside of the academic and economics environments I am used to. There were a lot of interesting presentations, and a lot of good books were mentioned along the way.</p>
<p>First, a couple of blasts from the past: Claude Hopkins’s <em>Scientific Advertising</em> (if the one dollar Amazon price is prohibitive, it doesn’t take much searching to find some free pdf versions) and Vance Packard’s <em>The Hidden Persuaders</em>. The idea of injecting more science into advertising is not new.</p>
<p>The usual behavioural science texts got plenty of mentions, particularly Daniel Kahneman’s <a href="/posts/re-reading-kahnemans-thinking-fast-and-slow.html">Thinking, Fast and Slow</a>. System 1 and System 2 thinking were regular frames for the speakers (and in today’s workshops). Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein’s <a href="/posts/thaler-and-sunsteins-nudge.html">Nudge</a> and Dan Ariely’s <a href="/posts/arielys-predictably-irrational.html">Predictably Irrational</a> also got the expected mentions.</p>
<p>The first three speakers had an evolutionary thread in parts of their talks (nice to see), so naturally a few books I have plugged before came up. Rory Sutherland put up his <a href="http://verge.ogilvydo.com/rory-sutherlands-reading-list">reading list from Verge</a>, which includes Paul Seabright’s <em>The Company of Strangers</em>, Jonathan Haidt’s <a href="/posts/haidts-the-righteous-mind.html">The Righteous Mind</a>, Robert Kurzban’s <em>Why Everyone (Else) Is a Hypocrite: Evolution and the Modular Mind</em> and Robert Frank’s <a href="/posts/franks-the-darwin-economy.html">The Darwin Economy</a>. All highly recommended, as is the rest of Sutherland’s reading pile, although I haven’t read Stuart Sutherland’s <em>Irrationality: the enemy within</em>, which I suppose will get added to my list.</p>
<p>Another book I had not come across before was Iain McGilchrist’s <em>The Master and His Emissary: The Divided Brain and the Making of the Western World</em>, which looks interesting.</p>
<p>Uri Gneezy and John List got a solid mention from the last presenter, Liam Smith from Monash University’s <a href="http://www.behaviourworksaustralia.org/">BehaviourWorks Australia</a>. Gneezy and List’s new book <em>The Why Axis: Hidden Motives and the Undiscovered Economics of Everyday Life</em> is also sitting on my reading pile.</p>
<p>Outside of the presentations, a few other interesting books came up in conversation. They included Jim Manzi’s <a href="/posts/manzis-uncontrolled.html">Uncontrolled: The Surprising Payoff of Trial-and-Error for Business, Politics, and Society</a>, which should be on your reading list. One of my favourite books, Christopher Buckley’s <em>Thank You for Smoking</em> was also mentioned, which was unsurprising considering the potential sin industry clients of many of the conference attendees – and if you do read it, rip out the last couple of pages. While Barry Schwartz’s book <a href="/posts/barry-schwartzs-the-paradox-of-choice-why-more-is-less.html">The Paradox of Choice</a> was not specifically mentioned, the phrase was regularly used.</p>
<p>Finally, Adam Ferrier, the conference curator, has a book out - <a href="/posts/notes-on-a-few-books.html">The Advertising Effect: How to Change Behaviour</a>. After organising a conference myself earlier in the year, I feel for him - many rewards but so much effort.</p>
