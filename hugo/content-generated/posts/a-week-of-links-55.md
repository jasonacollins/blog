<p>Links this week:</p>
<ol type="1">
<li><p>There are plenty of reviews of Nicholas Wade’s new book, <em>A Troublesome Inheritance: Genes, Race and Human History</em>. <a href="http://www.realclearscience.com/articles/2014/05/06/race_is_real_what_does_that_mean_for_society_108642.html">Robert VerBruggen</a>’s is one of the more interesting. We’ll be throwing a lot of social science under the bus if we apply <a href="http://www.slate.com/articles/health_and_science/science/2014/05/troublesome_inheritance_critique_nicholas_wade_s_dated_assumptions_about.html">Andrew Gelman’s filter</a> more generally.</p></li>
<li><p><a href="http://andrewgelman.com/2014/05/08/discussion-steven-pinker-research-attached-data-noisy-essentially-uninformative/">Gelman again</a>, this time on poor research in evolutionary psychology. I agree with both him and Pinker here.</p></li>
<li><p>A review of <a href="http://www.businessweek.com/articles/2014-05-08/book-review-think-like-a-freak-by-steven-levitt-and-stephen-dubner">Think Like a Freak</a>. From the excerpts I have seen, it seems that Levitt and Dubner are running into the same problem as a lot of the behavioural economics literature - there are only so many “funky” stories to go around.</p></li>
<li><p><a href="http://www.economist.com/news/science-and-technology/21601809-potent-source-genetic-variation-cognitive-ability-has-just-been">Can a single gene explain 3 per cent of the variation in IQ</a>? I don’t think so, but interesting none the less.</p></li>
