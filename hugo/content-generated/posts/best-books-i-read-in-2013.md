<p>As is my habit, each year I give a list of the best books I have read during the year. I tend not to focus on the newest releases, so most of the list was not published this year. In no particular order:</p>
<ol type="1">
<li><p>Paul Frijters and Gigi Foster’s <a href="/posts/an-economic-theory-of-greed-love-groups-and-networks.html">An Economic Theory of Greed, Love, Groups, and Networks</a> (see also <a href="/posts/the-love-principle.html">here</a>): I don’t buy into many of the arguments, but the most interesting book I read all year.</p></li>
<li><p>Richard Nelson and Sidney Winter’s <a href="/posts/nelson-and-winters-an-evolutionary-theory-of-economic-change.html">An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change</a>: The book that kickstarted evolutionary economics as a serious pursuit. Although slightly dated, a great example of how to critique mainstream economics.</p></li>
<li><p>Steven Pinker’s <em>The Better Angels of Our Nature: Why Violence Has Declined</em> (no review yet): Pinker’s case is compelling and important.</p></li>
<li><p>Oded Galor’s <a href="/posts/galors-unified-growth-theory.html">Unified Growth Theory</a>: Another book for which I’m not completely onboard with the central arguments, but I love the ambition and ideas.</p></li>
<li><p>I read a lot of classics this year. I thought Victor Hugo’s Les Miserables was great. I loved the use of language in Vladimir Nabokov’s Lolita. But ultimately, I enjoyed Jules Verne’s <em>Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea</em> the most (an early seasteader?).</p></li>
</ol>
<p>Books I read that didn’t make the list but are worth a mention include Nate Silver’s <a href="/posts/silvers-the-signal-and-the-noise.html">The Signal and the Noise: Why So Many Predictions Fail but Some Don’t</a> (I thought a couple of chapters were great, but just wasn’t that excited by a lot of it) and Victor Hwang and Greg Horowitt’s <a href="/posts/hwang-and-horowitts-the-rainforest.html">The Rainforest: The Secret to Building the Next Silicon Valley</a>.</p>
