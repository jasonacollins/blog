<p>Dan Ariely’s <em>Predictably Irrational</em> is one of the most commonly referenced popular books on behavioural science. It describes Ariely’s experiments in the areas of cheating, procrastination, social norms, hot decision-making and so on.</p>
<p>My first impression of the book when I read it back in 2013 was that it was fantastic that Ariely described his own experiments. In the behavioural science literature, you often come across the same experiments over and over. The field doesn’t feel very deep. But this is easier to deal with when the person is describing their own experiments and they can give the experiments colour beyond the content of the published papers. Then again, if you have listened to a lot of Ariely’s lectures via podcast (as I have), much of the book will be familiar territory.</p>
<p>With the benefit of hindsight, however, we can see that this supposedly nice element of Ariely describing his own experiments was a major flaw. Ariely made up the data for many of his experiments. He is a fraud. For example, if work reported in <em>Predictably Irrational</em>, students did not <a href="https://datacolada.org/138">perform better with artificial deadlines</a>. Work reported in his later books is similarly fraudulent.</p>
<p>That said, on the initial read I did find some interesting threads.</p>
<p>First was his framing of how the link between price and demand flows in two directions. People do not simply demand a certain quantity at a certain price, as the price may provide information about how desirable a product is. Ariely opens that section with the story of how James Assael created demand for black pearls. After initially failing in his marketing attempts, he placed the pearls in the store of a gemstone dealer friend with an outrageously high price relative to the previous prices at which he had failed to sell any of the pearls. Accompanied by an advertising campaign, the demand was born.</p>
<p>Ariely’s explanation of this feedback between price and demand relates to our need for arbitrary coherence. Since we do not know what many things are worth, we will seek an anchor - say a previous price or the last two digits on our social security card. Once that anchor is created, this is the benchmark against which value is measured. Placing the pearls in the gemstone store and advertisements created an anchor that still exists.</p>
<p>A second interesting thread was the desire for individuality. Ariely relates an experiment where he offered a selection of beers to pub patrons as free samples. Where people ordered in sequence and aloud so that each member of a group heard what the others ordered, they tended to order different beers. With private ordering they tended to cluster more. This occurred because those who ordered last avoided the beers that people in their group had ordered before them. So, if you feel a need to express individuality through your meal or drink order, get in first. Of course, whether this result is true or not is another matter.</p>
<p>Finally, Ariely’s work on hot decision-making, where his team provided computers loaded with arousing pictures to experimental subjects, is amusing. Did it actually occur?</p>
<p>So given what has been revealed since Predictably Irrational was published, I wouldn’t bother reading it. That is, unless you want a bit of amusement and you don’t care if anything he says is true.</p>
