It's rare to find a book on behavioural economics that can both pick at the flaws without strawmanning 

That said, Rizzo and Whitman have provided possibly the most thorough and entertaining critique of the body of evidence underlying 

I have many quibbles with the book, largely arising from it's one sided nature.

One is the question of who the behavioural economists are. In many cases, Rizzo and Whitman will present what they believe to be the behavioural economics canon on a particular topic. They will then cite many papers calling into question those papers and the conclusions of the behavioural economists. But those papers are by behavioural economists too!

Another is their failure to examine any of the evidence on how applied behavioural economics occurs in practice. 

Their inability to note even a single example of acceptable "paternalism" is also an omission. As two examples, let me suggest reminders and simplifying the process for admissions. 

Benchmark of perfection - unless you have precise estimates for the magnitudes of effects, cannot optimise the sin tax or whatever parameter you want to optimise.

Don't always call on the strongest evidence. Will cite the behavioural economics finding, the Gigerenzer response, but then ignorant to the rejoinders.

Extends to selection of studies. Happy to cite replication crisis at times but many places rely on debunked studies / concepts to make points - e.g. willpower. Use priming studies that I am sure are ridiculous after spending a rather large footnote describing the lack of power in the priming studies in Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow.
