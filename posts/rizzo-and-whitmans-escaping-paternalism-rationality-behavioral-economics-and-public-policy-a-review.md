It's rare to find a book on behavioural economics that can pick at the flaws while presenting the best case for the other side. Mario J. Rizzo and Glen Whitman's Escaping Paternalism: Rationality, Behavioral economics, and Public Policy is not that rare book. While excellent in many dimensions, there is barely a point in the book where they provide the strongest argument from their behavioural economist foils.

That said, Rizzo and Whitman have provided possibly the most thorough and entertaining critique of the application of behavioural economics to public policy.

Their argument is multi-pronged. They disagree with the normative benchmark against which human decisions and behaviour are assessed. Alternative interpretations of the experimental results in the literatures on heuristics, biases and beliefs suggest that many of the supposedly irrational behaviours are superior in real-world contexts. Further, this literature is full of ambiguity and lacks generalisability, leading it to be insufficient to guide policy making. People are often aware of their constraints, so they can counteract or control them. There is a knowledge problem in that policy makers don't (and in fact, can't) know the preferences of those whose behaviour they seek to change. To top it off, the paternalist policy-makers would be subject to the same biases as everyone else (not to mention facing incentives that don't necessarily align with citizen welfare), hampering the development of good policy and setting us on the slippery slope to more restrictive paternalism. 

I am sympathetic to all of those threads of argument, and Rizzo and Whitman provide a strong foundation to each. They cover a broad literature that a shallow dip into behavioural economics (e.g. via reading Daniel Kahneman's [Thinking, Fast and Slow](re-reading-kahnemans-thinking-fast-and-slow.md) or Dan Ariely's [Predictably Irrational](dan-arielys-predictably-irrational.md)) would not reveal exists. The knowledge problem is real. We often have a poor conception of why people act the way they do.

However, Rizzo and Whitman are one-sided in their arguments. Their preferred definition of rationality - labelled "inclusive rationality" - is so loose that almost any behaviour can be defined as inclusively rational. Their coverage of the literature, while pointing to important critiques of the experiments, normally stops short of then showing the strongest responses. They cite the replication issues to undermine some studies yet happily cite work that has failed replication or is almost certainly flaky.

Their choice of paternalistic case studies excludes much of the policy work that might better withstand their critiques. They also demand near-perfect knowledge of how people will respond before any policy goes ahead. That standard would prevent almost any public policy that requires an understanding of what people value. How could you estimate the effect of a tax? Or set a polluter pays policy?

The heart of their argument is that people are "inclusively rational". Rationality is not conformance to a set of assumptions or rules of logic. Inclusive rationality includes experimenting with choices, preference discovery and learning. It involves structuring your environment, possibly for purposes of self-control. While these don't involve what Rizzo and Whitman call "puppet" rationality, they are sensible behaviours for real people.

It's a nice idea, and it would have been great if they had decided to rigorously define it and test the boundaries, but through the book it serves more as a lazy fallback than a topic of serious exploration. For example, when discussing action bias deriving from an excessive willingness to attribute causality to one's own actions, they offhandedly comment that "Even this mental process might be deemed broadly rational, once we allow for beliefs that have direct utility to the believer." Once you're at this degree of flexibility, everything is inclusively rational!

One question I kept asking was: who exactly are these behavioural economists? Rizzo and Whitman often state the behavioural economics canon on a topic, then cite papers that challenge it. But those challenges are usually by behavioural economists too! In many cases this is simply the typical passage of time - people propose ideas, future papers challenge and refine them.

For example, they describe quasi-hyperbolic discounting as the default option for analysing anomalies over time, then include work by David Laibson - one of hyperbolic discounting's main supporters - along with other behavioural economists, in rebuttal. Similarly, the evidence that auto-enrolment in retirement savings - the “iconic” case for behavioural paternalism - delivers less than it seems comes largely from Beshears, Choi, Laibson and Madrian, the group behind much of the early evidence on default effects. The debate about loss aversion is also largely (although not exclusively) one between behavioural economists. (There is also a point where they concede that many of these claims come from "older and more popular" analyses - meaning many of their "behavioural economists believe X" are better read as "Richard Thaler or some other populariser stated X".) 

Many of these threads stop at a convenient place for their case against the behavioural economist view. They will cite the initial behavioural economics finding, the critical response, but then we often don't see the rejoinders.

Their examination of the Linda problem and the associated conjunction fallacy is a good example. After being given a description of Linda designed to be representative of a feminist, people are asked (in various ways) which is more probable: that Linda is a bank teller or that Linda is a bank teller who is active in the feminist movement. Many people respond the latter, even though the latter cannot be more probable than the former as it is a conjunction involving the former. There are many good reasons to critique this experiment, as Gerd Gigerenzer and others have done. Maybe they interpreted "more probable" differently to the strict rules of logic. Rizzo and Whitman cover this well. But there are experiments that deal with many of the objections. For instance, in one experiment people bet real money on a die with four green and two red faces. About two-thirds of participants picked GRGRRR over RGRRR, despite the first containing the second making the first less likely. There was no ambiguous wording - just a bet. Rizzo and Whitman don't mention this work (a sampling of which can be found [here](debating-the-conjunction-fallacy.md)).

This pattern repeats through most sections. Rizzo and Whitman discuss [Gal and Rucker's argument against loss aversion](the-case-against-loss-aversion.md), even citing a commentary in support, while ignoring the critical commentaries. They borrow [Don Moore's categories of overconfidence](the-three-faces-of-overconfidence.md) (although only in Chapter 8 when critiquing policy makers, not in Chapter 5 when making sweeping claims), without mentioning any of his findings about the stability of various forms of overconfidence. They cite the replication crisis when it suits them. A footnote describes how the priming studies in Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow* were grossly underpowered, yet their case for counteractive self-control uses studies that prime students to think about distractions. They rely on Roy Baumeister's model of willpower as a resource that can be exhausted, despite the failed replications.

Presenting these debates does support their arguments urging caution in applying these ideas to policy. There is much uncertainty. But the evidential omissions leave a sour taste.

Another major omission is Rizzo and Whitman's failure to examine any of the evidence on how applied behavioural economics occurs in practice. They know the Behavioural Insights Team (BIT) exists - they mention it in their public choice analysis of the issue - but don't bother to reference how they work or what types of interventions they roll out. If they did, they might notice that BIT's work generally involves observation phases where they try to see what people actually do and understand the heterogeneity of behaviour. They recognise that there are many possible outcomes and that there can be surprises, which is why they are field trial driven. It's likely the most empirical form of policy you can find (alongside development economics). It also undercuts Rizzo and Whitman's treatment of Cass Sunstein’s call for more randomised trials as mere rhetoric. The applied units are running those trials.

That is not to say there is nothing to criticise here. There is. I have criticised the Behavioural Insights Team for many of the things identified by Rizzo and Whitman: too quick to jump to biases, assuming what people want, working largely on "left-wing" coded issues, and so on. But to read Rizzo and Whitman you wouldn't even know that the on-the-ground approaches are much richer than a few behavioural economists spouting ideas in articles and books, which are then blindly implemented by policy makers.

And what of the types of interventions implemented by applied units like the BIT? Let's take one of their most famous: social norms on tax payment letters. Stating 9 out of 10 people pay on time increases on time payment. Rizzo and Whitman might argue this is inclusive rationality - there is information in that social norm! Maybe. They might also respond that this isn’t paternalism as the letter serves the public purse, not the taxpayer. Fair enough. But they name David Halpern’s Inside the Nudge Unit as a leading work of behavioural paternalism, then never look at what the unit does. Applied behavioural practitioners aren't just interested in behaviour that can be classified as irrational. They're interested in behaviour generally. If you walk through the reams of BIT reports, you'll see things like personalised reminder messages. Commitment devices for job seekers. Friction removal. Are these objectionable under Rizzo and Whitman's framework? Job seekers setting their own plans is the kind of self-imposed commitment Rizzo and Whitman praise in Chapter 6. Their inability to endorse even a single example of acceptable behavioural public policy is a stark omission. 

The discussion of default enrolment in retirement savings plans is one section where their selection of case-studies is one-sided. They choose auto-enrolment in retirement savings, which has been suggested by behavioural economists as a way to overcome present bias in people's retirement planning. I have sympathy for their critique - I personally believe Australia's mandatory retirement savings should be abolished - but they skip the obvious test of their own framework: Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow. In its original form, people are given the choice to opt in to a retirement plan where a fraction of any future pay rise is funnelled into retirement savings. It addresses loss aversion, in that take-home pay never goes down, and present bias, in that commitments are in the future. Once in, status quo bias keeps them there. There is plenty to debate around the precise way that it works, but it would seem to be an interesting policy response, providing a commitment device of sorts for workers to use. It is exactly the kind of self-imposed commitment device that Rizzo and Whitman champion. Yet it gets no discussion. They know the paper exists - it sits in the reference list - but it is uncited in the text.

The result of all this is a good book burdened by an uneven treatment of the material. It could have been great - and many parts are - but use it to absorb the general argument rather than as a thorough and well-balanced critique of behavioural economics in public policy.
